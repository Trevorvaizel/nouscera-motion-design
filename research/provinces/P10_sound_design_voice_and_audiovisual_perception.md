# THE SOUND, VOICE AND AUDIOVISION CARTOGRAPHER'S MANIFESTO
## A Map of How a Sound Changes What an Image Seems to Be, and Why Narration Is Not a Neutral Carrier

> The finished shot is not the discipline. Its visible success is only the residue of decisions about relationships, time, perception and consequence. We investigate why this practice works, which breakdowns shaped it, and what the studio may responsibly carry across.

**Research posture.** This document follows a shared cartographic architecture but not a transferred list of KIRA forces. It uses domain-specific scholarship, historical practice and source-specific limitations. Terms labelled “candidate force” are our causal hypotheses. They are not direct quotations from researchers, established universal laws or approved production standards. The archive has been researched as far as these sources support it; omissions and falsification questions remain open.

## PREAMBLE: THE DOMINANT MISUNDERSTANDING

Sound comes after picture. The scene is built, then we add voice, music, cinematic impacts and a few swells so the result feels professional.

Sound and image form an audiovisual event. Voice positions a knower, synchronization can bind unrelated signals, acoustic space shapes the imagined world, and music can alter event pacing and emotional interpretations. The film must also remain intelligible when playback is silent or hearing differs.

**Mastery is not:** Sound comes after picture. The scene is built, then we add voice, music, cinematic impacts and a few swells so the result feels professional

**Mastery is:** preserving the distinction between audiovisual added-value effect and synchrony-caused false unity, showing what observable evidence would change the decision, and justifying a creative choice rather than simply executing a technique.

**Governing question:** *Which audible relations make the changing visual world interpretable, emotionally precise and accessible without making sound an authority it has not earned?*

## PART I: THE FOUR LAYERS

### Layer 1: The Visible

Voice, effects, soundtrack, silence, captions, a rhythmic hit accompanying a white line.

**Dominant trap:** Sound is judged by whether the video feels more cinematic.

### Layer 2: The Mechanics

Sonic onset, spectral balance, prosody, synchronization, acoustic space, speech intelligibility and mixing.

**Dominant trap:** A sound cue is treated as mere reinforcement rather than part of perception.

### Layer 3: The Dynamics

Cross-modal integration, listener prior expectations, language, playback speaker, loudness context and accessibility change the film’s meaning.

**Dominant trap:** A strong soundtrack is presumed to work identically with every image and listener.

### Layer 4: The Invisible

Narrator authority, sonic assumptions about physical events, the right to infer causes from synchronization and the meaning accessible without hearing.

**Dominant trap:** An unsupported claim sounds true because the sonic treatment confers certainty.

## PART II: THE PROVINCE MAP AND KNOWLEDGE BOUNDARIES

**Native territory.** Sound Design, Voice and Audiovisual Perception. Within the studio, its operational home is **Sound / Narration**. These are working boundaries, not claims of disciplinary independence from film studies, design or psychology.

### Subprovinces that deserve independent study

- Film sound theory and audio-vision
- Audiovisual speech and multisensory integration

- Narrator position, prosody and rhetorical voice
- Soundscape, spatial acoustics and imagined off-screen world
- Musical rhythm and visual music
- Speech clarity, dynamics and technical loudness
- Captioning, silence and audio description

### Distinctions that must not collapse

- Voice volume differs from voice authority.
- A music swell is not a fact.
- Synchrony can create apparent unity where causality is absent.
- Audio-only comprehension differs from video-only comprehension.
- A narration track is not simply a literal transcript.
- Loudness compliance differs from intelligibility.

## PART III: RECOVERED SCAR TISSUE, REVISIONS AND DISPUTES

These traces are derived from the cited discipline rather than generated by analogy to the KIRA manifests. They mark where a once-plausible surface belief encounters an exception, revision or technical limit.

### Trace 1: Sound changed the nature of cinema, not just its decoration

Michel Chion’s *Audio-Vision* argues that sound/image together produce a transformed perception and that film speech remains central. [R1]

**What this changes in our studio:** The sound department belongs in conceptual design, not just postproduction polish.

### Trace 2: Seeing and hearing can change one another’s perception

The McGurk and sound-induced flash demonstrations show cross-modal integration effects under controlled stimuli. [R2][R3]

**What this changes in our studio:** We must not generalize the magnitude of these illusions to every cinematic effect; the existence of integration is what transfers.

### Trace 3: Experimental animation can take its pulse from music

NFB documents *Begone Dull Care* as graphic direct-film animation with the Oscar Peterson Trio. [R4]

**What this changes in our studio:** Musical motion is an artistic mode, not a guarantee of explanatory clarity.

### Trace 4: Sound has accessibility and engineering obligations

W3C standards address synchronized captions including meaningful non-speech sounds; ITU BS.1770 specifies measurement algorithms for programme loudness and true-peak. [R5][R6]

**What this changes in our studio:** Technical loudness is a measurement requirement, not an artistic target loudness for all social media.

## PART IV: CANDIDATE NATIVE FORCES AND FAILURE TESTS

**Force promotion test.** Each entry below must identify a specific operating relation, a condition of failure, a better diagnostic action and a way in which the hypothesis might not hold. Forces with roots in general psychology or rhetoric remain *cross-domain mechanisms in cinematic use*, not magically “native” because we renamed them. An individual positive test is not a universal validation.

### FORCE 1: Audiovisual added-value effect

**Surface pattern.** A visually neutral event feels ominous after a low drone or impact.

**Invisible mechanism.** Chion describes sound-image relations in which sound changes how the visual whole is perceived. [R1]

**Second-order consequence.** The studio can accidentally impose the emotion it wished to discover.

**Decision-changing application.** Test key scenes silent, with intended sound, and with a contrasting cue.

**Failure mode.** A route revelation becomes a threat because of an ominous swell.

**Field test and limiting condition.** Viewers infer the intended emotional register without unjustified fear.

### FORCE 2: Synchrony-caused false unity

**Surface pattern.** An object pulses with a sound and appears to be causing or responding to it.

**Invisible mechanism.** Cross-modal perception can bind close-in-time signals, as sensory illusions demonstrate in controlled settings. [R2][R3]

**Second-order consequence.** A synced sound cue can imply causal linkage where only coincidence exists.

**Decision-changing application.** Specify whether synchronization means literal event, metaphor or punctuation.

**Failure mode.** A false answer lights up with a reassuring bell and seems validated.

**Field test and limiting condition.** Viewers do not attribute unsupported causal relationships to sonic alignment.

### FORCE 3: Narrator authority coupling

**Surface pattern.** A confident voice makes a speculative illustration sound like objective fact.

**Invisible mechanism.** Narration has speaker identity, certainty, rhythm and credibility cues; Chion’s voice-centered analysis and documentary scholarship distinguish voice from evidentiary truth. [R1][R7]

**Second-order consequence.** The sonic medium installs authority beyond the cited claim.

**Decision-changing application.** Audit voice tense, confidence, qualifications, impersonation and source warrant.

**Failure mode.** “This is why everyone fails” is spoken as a law without evidence.

**Field test and limiting condition.** Viewers can identify what is interpretation rather than fact.

### FORCE 4: Verbal intelligibility priority

**Surface pattern.** Music and impacts mask a key phrase.

**Invisible mechanism.** Auditory streams compete, and processing a spoken distinction requires enough signal and temporal space. [R1]

**Second-order consequence.** The film remains spectacular but a sentence that carries the argument is lost.

**Decision-changing application.** Test narration in realistic playback conditions and with sound effects disabled.

**Failure mode.** A bass rise arrives under the phrase “not information, but orientation.”

**Field test and limiting condition.** Listeners accurately repeat and explain the intended contrast.

### FORCE 5: Sonic spatialization and off-screen world

**Surface pattern.** The black void has no perceived location or scale until sound provides distance cues.

**Invisible mechanism.** Reverberation, spectral shaping and source location can suggest a space larger than the visible frame; Chion discusses how sound extends the audiovisual scene. [R1]

**Second-order consequence.** An impressive soundscape can invent a world contradictory to the visual geometry.

**Decision-changing application.** Specify distance, perspective and acoustic material only where useful.

**Failure mode.** A close scratch suddenly sounds like a cathedral impact without a scene change.

**Field test and limiting condition.** Viewers describe a consistent apparent spatial relation.

### FORCE 6: Sound-motion rhythm interdependence

**Surface pattern.** Visual motion and spoken cadence run on incompatible beats.

**Invisible mechanism.** Visual music traditions demonstrate deliberate rhythmic composition of sight and sound, while narrative speech has its own syntactic and breathing rhythms. [R4]

**Second-order consequence.** Transition moments become cluttered or anticlimactic.

**Decision-changing application.** Construct a shared timing score marking phonetic emphasis, motion acceleration and rests.

**Failure mode.** A visual climax arrives while a dependent clause is still unfinished.

**Field test and limiting condition.** The conceptual punctuation is perceived without requiring a hard music hit.

### FORCE 7: Silence as a state, not a gap

**Surface pattern.** The soundtrack fills every moment to avoid dead air.

**Invisible mechanism.** The contrast between activity and absence can structure attention and expectation, but its interpretation depends on established sonic context. [R1]

**Second-order consequence.** Constant sound removes the ability to distinguish recognition from atmosphere.

**Decision-changing application.** Treat silence, near-silence and breath as intentional choices with playback constraints.

**Failure mode.** The final single path is revealed under an unchanged loud cinematic bed.

**Field test and limiting condition.** The reveal’s timing and meaning remain readable with sparse sound.

### FORCE 8: Equivalent access paths

**Surface pattern.** The argument disappears for a viewer who cannot hear the voice or see the central transformation.

**Invisible mechanism.** Captions and audio description serve different access requirements, including conveying meaningful sound effects and visual information. [R5][R8]

**Second-order consequence.** A film becomes accessible in interface terms but not in meaning.

**Decision-changing application.** Conduct visual-only, captioned and audio-described reconstruction tests.

**Failure mode.** The words “this path” refer to an uncaptioned movement that cannot be identified.

**Field test and limiting condition.** The central distinction survives through available sensory routes.

## PART V: PERCEPTUAL STAGES OF THE PRACTITIONER

### Stage 0: The soundtrack decorator

Sees the surface as proof: Sound comes after picture. The scene is built, then we add voice, music, cinematic impacts and a few swells so the result feels professional.

### Stage 1: The voice and effects operator

Can use narrator authority statement but does not yet reliably diagnose why it worked.

### Stage 2: The audiovisual synchronization analyst

Distinguishes audiovisual added-value effect from synchrony-caused false unity and anticipates the different failures.

### Stage 3: The sonic narrative director

Tests opposed explanations using speech-under-music intelligibility check and designs an alternative that could disconfirm the first reading.

### Stage 4: The audio-vision and access cartographer

Can predict when equivalent access paths will undermine a result, defend a decision from source and viewer evidence, and revise the practice when its limits appear.

**The polished-output valley.** The practitioner may become fluent in producing attractive surfaces before becoming able to predict the domain-specific failure of audiovisual added-value effect. This is not resolved by generating more options; the discriminating operation has to be learned and tested.

## PART VII: CASE TRACES AND COUNTERCASES

These are evidence-bearing cases, not universal templates. Where a case is a hypothetical studio scene, it is explicitly identified as such.

### Case 1: Chion, *Audio-Vision*

**What the case establishes or exhibits.** Film scholarship treats sound as altering the images’ perceived character rather than being attached later. [R1]

**What it cannot establish.** A theory of audio-vision is not a formula to maximize music.

**Studio translation to test rather than assume.** Define sound in relation to the visual’s interpretation before sound selection.

### Case 2: The McGurk and flash illusions

**What the case establishes or exhibits.** Classic controlled demonstrations show that visual and auditory signals can influence perceived speech and visual events. [R2][R3]

**What it cannot establish.** They are not measurements of what a vortex sound effect will do to every audience.

**Studio translation to test rather than assume.** Treat audio onset as a binding hypothesis requiring viewer tests.

### Case 3: NFB, *Begone Dull Care*

**What the case establishes or exhibits.** Music and animated form share rhythm in a direct-film work. [R4]

**What it cannot establish.** Music-oriented visual form can lack an independent explanatory proposition; that is not failure for an abstract film.

**Studio translation to test rather than assume.** Separate our audio-visual aesthetic target from the learning objective.

## PART X: RESEARCH RETURN AND FIRST EXPERIMENTAL GATE

**Candidate comparison:** Show one identical animation with silence, minimal sound and a contrasting music cue. Ask which emotional and causal statements viewers inferred, then test captioned and audio-only versions.

**Evaluation receipt to preserve.** State (1) original idea and intended viewer change; (2) visual/audio/temporal invariant; (3) candidate treatments; (4) predicted difference; (5) observed viewer interpretation or model output; (6) a counterexample; (7) remaining uncertainty; (8) next justified revision. Nothing is accepted only because the film looks impressive.

**No tool lock-in.** AI generation is the preferred visual production method, with 2.5D or compositing when a particular operation requires control. We do not equate any vendor’s available preset with a timeless cinematic principle.

## CREED

> The decisive test of sound design, voice and audiovisual perception is not whether the output appears excellent. It is whether we can explain, anticipate and correct audiovisual added-value effect, without sacrificing the work the film is meant to do for its viewer.
