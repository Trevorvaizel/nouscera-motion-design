# THE COGNITIVE LOAD AND LEARNING SCIENCES CARTOGRAPHER'S MANIFESTO
## A Map of What Actually Changes in a Learner — and What the Teacher Cannot See

---

> The map is not the territory.
>
> But in learning, the lesson is not the change, the score is not the knowledge, and the fluent feeling is not the durable skill.
>
> Every classroom presents activity as learning. The master sees what survives when the support is gone.
>
> This document maps that change.

---

## PREAMBLE: THE DOMINANT MISUNDERSTANDING

You believe learning is what was taught.

Content moves from a container called curriculum into a container called learner. If it was presented clearly, it was received; if the learner was active and engaged, they learned; if the score is high, the knowledge is there. Teaching is delivery, and learning is reception.

This is what the surface teaches, because the surface is the activity — the busy room, the completed worksheet, the confident nod, the good grade produced with help.

But learning is not exposure, activity, completion, confidence, enjoyment, or a score produced with support. It is a change in what a person can later retrieve, explain, discriminate, adapt, or use. And the unit is never the material alone or the learner alone, but the relation among prior knowledge, task, representation, activity, support, timing, social setting, and assessment.

**Mastery is NOT:**
- Believing presentation equals learning
- Reading immediate fluency or confidence as durable knowledge
- Treating "cognitive load" as a fixed number of items
- Assuming more support, or more difficulty, is always better
- Mistaking a polished AI-assisted artifact for a capable learner

**Mastery IS:**
- Separating performance, durable learning, transfer, field independence, and authorship
- Reading demand as *relational* to prior knowledge, not as a property of the content
- Knowing which difficulties are desirable and which are just difficult
- Designing support that *fades*, and testing the unaided return
- Asking what the person can do when the teacher, prompt, and AI are gone

Three tempting laws must be refused at the outset: there is no universal *prediction-error gate*; no instructional *4 ± 1* threshold above which "no encoding occurs"; and no sharp *embodiment split*. Preserving these qualifications while keeping the field usable is the whole discipline.

## PART I: THE FOUR LAYERS OF LEARNING REALITY

### Layer 1: The Visible
Lessons, worksheets, activities, discussions, scores, dashboards, badges, portfolios, completed tasks.
**What lives here:** the artifacts and behaviors people call "learning."
**The trap:** activity is observable and constructive operation is not — a busy room can produce nothing durable.

### Layer 2: The Mechanics
Selection, representation, sequence, modeling, guidance, rehearsal, retrieval, comparison, feedback, assessment inference.
**What lives here:** the operations that might produce change.
**The trap:** mechanics without dynamics produce technicians who run a technique and cannot see why it helped one learner and burdened the next.

### Layer 3: The Dynamics
Prior knowledge, teacher interpretation, learner uptake and resistance, washback, workload, timing, and the recursive change in what the learner can do.
**What lives here:** why the same design produces different learning across learners and rooms.
**The trap:** dynamics without invisible forces produce analysts who see techniques vary and cannot say which forces recur.

### Layer 4: The Invisible
Schema-relative demand, the difference between performance and durable capability, the geometry of transfer cues, the authorship boundary, and the institutional conditions that decide what a technique becomes.
**What lives here:** the native forces of Part III.
When a lesson "worked" but the capability vanished, the failure lives here.

What changes in a learner, for whom, under which conditions, and how the change is measured — spanning cognitive architecture, instruction, memory, transfer, motivation, and implementation.

## PART III: THE ELEVEN NATIVE FORCES

### FORCE I: Schema-Relative Demand
**Surface Pattern:** the same task is trivial for one learner and impossible for another.
**Invisible Mechanism:** demand is element interactivity *relative to prior knowledge*; retrievable schemas compress relations and guide selection (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas; Baddeley; Cowan; Ericsson & Kintsch).
**Second-Order Force:** pacing and grouping needs diverge; "this lesson has seven elements, so it exceeds capacity" is a category error.
**Application:** pretest relevant knowledge; pretrain components; vary support.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when content is called "too hard," diagnose the learner-relative element interactivity, not the material.
**Transplant Test:** "know your audience" is generic; the specific mechanism — *element interactivity relative to schemas* setting working-memory demand — is native to cognitive load theory.
**Failure Mode:** labeling content itself "high load."

### FORCE II: Means–Ends Search Displacement
**Surface Pattern:** novices solve problems yet cannot explain the reusable structure.
**Invisible Mechanism:** means–ends search maintains moves and dead ends instead of encoding relations among steps; a worked example redirects attention to structure (Sweller).
**Second-Order Force:** heavy effort is mistaken for productive struggle.
**Application:** compare worked examples, completion problems, and guided practice, then fade support.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when effort is high but learning low, check whether search displaced schema-building.
**Transplant Test:** "struggle isn't always learning" is loose; the specific mechanism — *means–ends search* consuming capacity that would build schemas — is native to problem-solving research.
**Failure Mode:** concluding all problem-solving is harmful.

### FORCE III: Guidance–Expertise Reversal
**Surface Pattern:** an explanation that helps a novice slows or irritates an expert.
**Invisible Mechanism:** support that duplicates retrievable knowledge must still be coordinated with the task (Kalyuga's expertise-reversal effect).
**Second-Order Force:** static "best practice" becomes inequitable across expertise; age, status, and confidence are false proxies for knowledge.
**Application:** add expertise checks and evidence-triggered fading.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when guidance backfires, check the learner's prior knowledge before the technique.
**Transplant Test:** "tailor to level" is generic; the specific mechanism — *redundant guidance imposing coordination load on the knowledgeable* — is native to CLT.
**Failure Mode:** a permanent scaffold applied across expertise.

### FORCE IV: Representation Coordination Cost
**Surface Pattern:** adding a diagram, animation, or second notation makes learning worse.
**Invisible Mechanism:** split attention, transient information, or duplication forces search and translation across sources (Mayer's multimedia principles).
**Second-Order Force:** "more visual" is mistaken for more accessible; learning-styles matching does not solve coordination (Pashler et al.).
**Application:** signal mappings, colocate dependencies, segment, pretrain notation, remove decorative competition.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a richer representation fails, check the coordination cost it added.
**Transplant Test:** "good visuals help" is loose; the specific mechanism — *cross-representation coordination* consuming resources for integration — is native to multimedia learning.
**Failure Mode:** modality preference treated as the fix.

### FORCE V: Retrieval Reconstruction
**Surface Pattern:** restudy feels fluent while later recall fails; testing feels harder while later access improves.
**Invisible Mechanism:** reconstructing target relations strengthens later access and cue routes (Roediger & Karpicke).
**Second-Order Force:** confidence becomes better calibrated when prediction meets evidence.
**Application:** low-shame recall, explanation, or decision reconstruction across delays, with feedback.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when fluency is high, test delayed unaided recall before crediting learning.
**Transplant Test:** "practice helps" is generic; the specific mechanism — *effortful retrieval* strengthening later access more than restudy — is native to memory research.
**Failure Mode:** high-stakes quizzing or repetition without correction.

### FORCE VI: Spacing–Difficulty Inversion
**Surface Pattern:** massed practice looks efficient but fades; spaced practice feels slower but lasts.
**Invisible Mechanism:** later, separated encounters require renewed retrieval and diversify temporal cues (Bjork's desirable difficulties).
**Second-Order Force:** systems optimized for smooth completion underinvest in return.
**Application:** schedule cumulative returns with feedback and adaptive intervals.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when performance dips during study, ask whether it is desirable difficulty or impossible difficulty.
**Transplant Test:** "space it out" is generic; the specific mechanism — *desirable difficulty* trading present fluency for durable access — is native to learning science.
**Failure Mode:** any long interval treated as beneficial, or spacing material never understood.

### FORCE VII: Learning–Performance Dissociation
**Surface Pattern:** the higher-immediate-score condition produces weaker delayed results; students feel they learned less when they learned more.
**Invisible Mechanism:** support, restudy, and fluent presentation raise present accessibility without equally durable retrieval structures (Deslauriers et al.).
**Second-Order Force:** dashboards reward the wrong intervention.
**Application:** pair immediate measures with delayed, unaided, and transfer tests.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a technique "wins" immediately, delay the test before trusting it.
**Transplant Test:** "results can mislead" is loose; the specific mechanism — a *dissociation between current performance and durable learning* — is native to memory/instruction research.
**Failure Mode:** feeling of learning mistaken for learning.

### FORCE VIII: Transfer Cue Geometry
**Surface Pattern:** a learner knows a principle in one form but does not recognize it elsewhere.
**Invisible Mechanism:** transfer needs both an abstracted invariant and cues that signal applicability; source and destination differ in domain, context, function, modality, social setting (Barnett & Ceci).
**Second-Order Force:** "near/far" becomes a misleading single distance.
**Application:** specify the changed dimensions, compare cases, practice cue detection, assess future learning.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when knowledge stays inert, map which cues the destination lacks.
**Transplant Test:** "apply what you learn" is generic; the specific mechanism — *applicability cues plus invariant* across specified transfer dimensions — is native to transfer research.
**Failure Mode:** training only surface variety, or testing with obvious researcher cues.

### FORCE IX: Metacognitive Cue Capture
**Surface Pattern:** ease, speed, familiarity, or confident feedback inflates judgments of learning.
**Invisible Mechanism:** regulation follows available cues even when those cues poorly predict later access.
**Second-Order Force:** attractive systems win trust without building capability.
**Application:** prediction, unaided attempt, feedback, revised confidence.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when learners feel done, check whether fluency, not evidence, told them so.
**Transplant Test:** "confidence misleads" is loose; the specific mechanism — *metacognitive monitoring captured by non-diagnostic cues* — is native to metacognition research.
**Failure Mode:** premature stopping on a fluency signal.

### FORCE X: Affective-Control Bandwidth
**Surface Pattern:** threat, shame, or low control narrows attention, persistence, and error disclosure.
**Invisible Mechanism:** worry and monitoring occupy executive resources; emotion changes approach, avoidance, and help-seeking (Pekrun; Ryan & Deci).
**Second-Order Force:** silence is misread as incapacity.
**Application:** lower humiliation, clarify control, preserve meaningful challenge, normalize correction.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a learner disengages, check the affective conditions before the ability.
**Transplant Test:** "emotions matter" is generic; the specific mechanism — *evaluative threat consuming control resources* in a learning task — is native to educational affect research.
**Failure Mode:** claiming a fixed amount of cognition is "consumed," or making all challenge emotionally neutral.

### FORCE XI: Offloading–Internalization Tension
**Surface Pattern:** a tool improves present output while unaided capability grows, stalls, or declines.
**Invisible Mechanism:** external action changes the task; whether internal knowledge grows depends on what the learner still retrieves, selects, explains, and corrects (Risko & Gilbert; Bastani et al.; Kestin et al.).
**Second-Order Force:** dependency is mistaken for augmentation, or useful support is rejected as cheating.
**Application:** record work allocation; require learner contribution; test unaided return.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when AI raises output, test delayed unaided performance before crediting learning.
**Transplant Test:** "tools can help or hurt" is generic; the specific mechanism — *offloading* relocating the very operations practice was meant to build — is native to distributed-cognition learning.
**Failure Mode:** artifact quality mistaken for learning; prohibition mistaken for pedagogy.

## PART IV: THE STAGES OF THE LEARNING-SCIENCES CARTOGRAPHER

### Stage 1: The Exposure Separator
**Inner voice:** "Presented is not learned."
Sees the gap between exposure and change. **Trap:** none yet named; the work begins. **Move:** name the learner operation and the outcome.

### Stage 2: The Outcome Distinguisher
**Inner voice:** "Performance, retention, transfer, independence, authorship differ."
Separates the five outcomes. **Trap:** measuring the easy one. **Move:** design measures at the right delay and support level.

### Stage 3: The Relational-Demand Reader
**Inner voice:** "Difficulty depends on prior knowledge."
Reads schema-relative demand. **Trap:** counting items. **Move:** diagnose element coordination, not item count.

### ⬇ THE PERFORMANCE VALLEY — most instructors and edtech builders plateau here.
The valley is comfortable: scores rise, students feel good, the demo dazzles. Nothing signals that the gains are support-dependent and vanish at delay, or that the fluent feeling is not learning. Crossing it means running the delayed, unaided, transfer test on your own best technique — and letting it lose.

### Stage 4: The Mechanism-Bound Designer
**Inner voice:** "Techniques work through mechanisms with boundaries."
Sees why a method helps and fails. **Trap:** generalizing a boundary-bound effect. **Move:** state failure conditions and evidence posture.

### Stage 5: The Adaptive-Support Architect
**Inner voice:** "Guidance changes value as knowledge changes."
Builds contingency and fading. **Trap:** removing help by schedule, not evidence. **Move:** build unaided-return tests.

### Stage 6: The Transfer & Ecology Mapper
**Inner voice:** "Capability must survive changed cues and institutions."
Sees transfer geometry and implementation systems. **Trap:** treating a lab effect as a classroom law. **Move:** specify destination cues, language, labor, materials, legitimacy.

### Stage 7: The Learner-Ownership Steward
**Inner voice:** "A design is incomplete until the person can act beyond it."
Preserves judgment and evidence of self-directed use; returns the learner to the field.

## PART V: THE TOOLKIT

**Tool 1 — Outcome Ledger.** Performance | durable learning | transfer | field independence | authorship, measured separately.
**Tool 2 — Prior-Knowledge Probe.** Pretest the schemas the task assumes.
**Tool 3 — Element-Interactivity Map.** What must be coordinated at once, relative to the learner.
**Tool 4 — Desirable-Difficulty Check.** Does this difficulty engage the target operation and stay recoverable?
**Tool 5 — Guidance-and-Fading Plan.** Target operation, initial support, removal signal, unaided attempt.
**Tool 6 — Representation Coordination Audit.** Split attention, transience, duplication, decorative competition.
**Tool 7 — Transfer Geometry Map.** Which dimensions change between source and destination.
**Tool 8 — Metacognitive Calibration Loop.** Predict, attempt unaided, feedback, revise confidence.
**Tool 9 — Delegation & Offload Ledger.** What the tool supplied, what the learner still did, what must survive removal.
**Tool 10 — Delayed Field-Return Test.** Change surface, delay support, move to a consequential setting; observe what survives.

## PART VI: DIAGNOSTICS

1. Which of the five outcomes is this design actually producing?
2. Is the demand a property of the content, or relative to this learner's schemas?
3. Is this difficulty desirable (engages the target, recoverable) or just difficult?
4. Does the support fade on evidence, or on a schedule?
5. What coordination cost did the added representation impose?
6. Which transfer cues does the destination lack?
7. Did fluency or evidence tell the learner they were done?
8. What did the tool supply, and what would survive its removal?
9. What delayed, unaided, transfer test would distinguish learning from performance?

## PART VII: CASE TRACES

**Sweller's worked examples** — means–ends search expends effort without building structure; worked examples redirect attention to relations. **Forces:** Means–Ends Displacement (II), Schema-Relative Demand (I). *Countercase:* examples can invite copying once schemas exist.

**Kalyuga's expertise reversal** — guidance useful to novices becomes redundant or harmful for the knowledgeable. **Forces:** Guidance–Expertise Reversal (III).

**Roediger & Karpicke's retrieval practice** — restudy led at 5 minutes; retrieval led after days, and restudy inflated confidence. **Forces:** Retrieval Reconstruction (V), Learning–Performance Dissociation (VII).

**Kapur's productive failure vs. non-replications** — a bounded generation attempt before instruction can help, but not universally; it turns on domain, design, consolidation. **Forces:** generative contrast within (I) and (VIII).

**Deslauriers et al.'s active learning** — active instruction produced more learning while students *felt* they learned less. **Forces:** Learning–Performance Dissociation (VII), Metacognitive Cue Capture (IX).

**Kenya PRIMR / Tusome (Piper et al.)** — the full package (guides + coaching + materials + monitoring) was the effective, cost-effective condition, not any single artifact. **Forces:** implementation coupling; complements as the real unit.

**Bastani vs. Kestin (AI)** — unguarded GPT raised practice and lowered unaided exams; a scaffolded tutor removed the harm and, elsewhere, improved measured learning. **Forces:** Offloading–Internalization Tension (XI).

## PART IX: THE PRESSURE-REPRESENTATION CROSSING

> K2M synthesis — learning science contributes the discipline of *return*.

A representation has not transferred sight because it feels accurate or produces a good supported response; ask what the person can later retrieve, discriminate, revise, and do when the map is absent. So: diagnose reception *relationally* (what relations are novel, what they already chunk, what competes for coordination) rather than counting facts or invoking a fixed mental window; use a lived scene or contrast to open attention without pretending it is the sole cause of change, then follow with retrieval, correction, and return; treat a card, map, or AI exchange as a *temporary cognitive surface* that shows what work it supplies and when control comes back; separate immediate relief from durable learning, transfer, and authorship; and keep the authorship boundary — a correct map that captures the person's judgment has failed the deeper purpose. The governing diagnostic: *what must this representation let the person notice and do now — and what evidence will show the sight became retrievable, transferable, and still theirs later?*

## CREED: THE LEARNING-SCIENCES CARTOGRAPHER'S OATH

I will not mistake exposure, activity, or a score for learning.

I will not read fluency or confidence as durable capability.

I will treat demand as relative to what the learner already knows.

I will use difficulty only where it engages the target and stays recoverable.

I will fade support on evidence, and test the unaided return.

I will separate performance, durable learning, transfer, independence, and authorship.

I will not let a tool become the hidden author of a learner's judgment.

I will price the institutional labor that makes a technique real.

I will return the learner to the field and ask what survives.

The proof is not that the artifact worked. It is that the person changed in a way the evidence can distinguish — and kept the right to direct that change.
