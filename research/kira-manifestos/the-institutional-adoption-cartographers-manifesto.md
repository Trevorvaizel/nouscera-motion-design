# THE INSTITUTIONAL ADOPTION CARTOGRAPHER'S MANIFESTO
## A Map of Why Institutions Say Yes, Why the Yes Means Nothing, and What the Territory Itself Cannot See

---

> The map is not the territory.
>
> But in adoption, the signature is not the commitment, the launch is not the practice, and the reach is not the value.
>
> Every institution presents a decision as a surface. The master sees the system the institution would have to become to use the thing well.
>
> This document maps that system.

---

## PREAMBLE: THE DOMINANT MISUNDERSTANDING

Most people trying to get something adopted are watching the wrong event.

They are polishing the demo, sharpening the pitch, and waiting for the moment a decision-maker says yes. They believe adoption is a *decision* — a good product meets a rational buyer, and if the product is good enough, the buyer commits.

This is not naïve. It is what the visible surface of the field shows them.

The visible surface of institutional adoption is: purchases, approvals, pilots, launches, logins, dashboards, and press releases. Every one of them looks like the end of the story. Every one of them is the beginning.

A person navigating by that surface is perpetually surprised. The superior product is refused and they call the institution irrational. The signed contract produces no working practice and they call it poor execution. The national rollout collapses into inequity and they call it bad luck.

**This manifesto is not about the yes.**

It is about the invisible grammar beneath the yes — the forces that decide what actually gets used, what quietly reverts, who carries the hidden cost, and what the master sees that the vendor cannot.

**Mastery of this field is NOT:**
- Winning the purchase decision
- Measuring user enthusiasm and calling it adoption
- Believing the best product wins
- Treating procurement as paperwork after the "real" decision
- Reading national reach as proof of quality

**Mastery of this field IS:**
- Seeing which *threshold* the institution is actually at, and what proof that threshold demands
- Knowing which actor carries the value, the burden, the risk, and the veto — and that they are rarely the same person
- Having a map that survives the launch, the champion's departure, the budget cut, and the model change
- Knowing what your dashboard cannot see
- Building the exit before you build the dependence

The field is not about persuading institutions to adopt. It is about understanding institutions so completely that the places where an adoption will live or die become visible before the money moves.

---

## PART I: THE FOUR LAYERS OF ADOPTION REALITY

### Layer 1: The Visible

What everyone can point at: the demo, the proposal, the signed contract, the launch email, the training day, the usage dashboard, the renewal.

This is where imitation begins. The novice sees a signed contract and believes adoption happened. The dashboard shows logins and they believe the practice is alive.

**What lives here:** RFPs, approvals, licenses, pilots, go-lives, seat counts, adoption curves, case studies, board decks.

**The trap:** every visible artifact is an *output* of deeper forces, and each one can be true while the adoption underneath is failing.

### Layer 2: The Mechanics

What can be taught directly: how procurement scores a bid, how a budget cycle gates timing, how a contract allocates data rights and exit terms, how an implementation plan sequences training and integration.

**What lives here:** total-cost-of-ownership models, contracting and SLAs, integration and interoperability, change-management steps, fidelity checklists, scale-up playbooks.

**The trap:** mechanics without dynamics produce administrators who can run a rollout by the book and cannot say why the same playbook delivered value in one school and burden in the next.

### Layer 3: The Dynamics

What changes with context: why a tool that saved time in the demo exports labor after launch, why a champion's exit strands a change, why a weaker standard wins on installed base, how defenders of the status quo rationally protect a viable routine.

**What lives here:** incentive conflict, professional jurisdiction, coalition and sponsorship, workflow repair, burden transfer, imitation under uncertainty, path dependence.

**The trap:** dynamics without invisible forces produce analysts who see that adoptions differ but cannot explain why the same forces recur across sectors, decades, and technologies.

### Layer 4: The Invisible

What generates the dynamics: the native forces that keep operating after every product, platform, and procurement rule is replaced.

**What lives here:** the native forces named in Part III; the structural reason value and burden land on different actors; the legitimacy pressures that make institutions adopt before evidence matures; the economics of complements, lock-in, and exit.

A force that cannot explain a *failed* adoption is not yet a force. A force that survives transplant to medicine or the military unchanged is not native to this domain.

## PART II: THE PROVINCE MAP

### Domain Boundary

The study of consequential collective commitment: how organizations and fields acquire, authorize, resource, implement, normalize, scale, sustain, and retire a practice — and how value, labor, risk, authority, and dependence are redistributed in the process.

### The In-Scope Provinces — a lifecycle that must not collapse

The field's native terrain is a *sequence of thresholds*, each with a different gatekeeper, a different proof, and a different way to fail. The recurring error is letting one threshold stand for the others.

1. **Diffusion** — spread through communication, networks, and imitation. Proves visibility, not use.
2. **Acceptance** — an individual's perceived usefulness, ease, and intention. Proves willingness, not commitment.
3. **Institutional commitment** — the organizational decision to acquire, authorize, resource, or mandate. Proves budget, not practice.
4. **Procurement** — price, contracting, data rights, interoperability, exit terms. *Fixes what can later be governed.*
5. **Implementation** — the work of putting the practice into operation. Proves effort, not routine.
6. **Normalization** — the practice becoming ordinary, legitimate work. Proves routine, not reach.
7. **Scale** — expansion of sites, populations, and institutional embedding. Proves reach, not quality or equity.
8. **Sustainment / exit** — continued valued function, or responsible retirement. Proves durability, not permanence.

**The actor constellation.** The unit is plural: the **purchaser** (money), the **gatekeeper** (permission and risk), the **implementer** (reorganizes work), the **user** (performs with the system), the **beneficiary** (receives the value), the **affected party** (bears consequences without choosing), the **maintainer** (carries continuity), the **accountable owner** (answers for failure). One person may wear several hats; no honest map assumes they coincide.

## PART III: THE EIGHT NATIVE FORCES

Each force has been run against the K2M Force Candidate Test: causal, explains failure, native (survives the transplant test by *not* moving unchanged to another domain), changes diagnosis, falsifiable. Masters are named where they own the mechanism; their titled works live in the Research Spine.

---

### FORCE I: The Counterfactual-Value Force

**Surface Pattern:** a proposal that looks obviously superior is refused, or a mediocre one is embraced, and observers call the institution irrational.

**Invisible Mechanism:** as Everett Rogers argued in *Diffusion of Innovations*, "relative advantage" is a perception formed against the *live alternative* — current practice, workaround, rival, internal build, or delay — never against nothing. The baseline, not the feature set, decides.

**Second-Order Force:** a wrongly assumed baseline makes rejection look like ignorance and sunk commitment look like prudence, so the vendor optimizes the wrong axis.

**Application:** before claiming advantage, state the actual counterfactual the institution is choosing among.

**Changes Diagnosis:** when a "better" product is refused, ask what baseline it was compared against before concluding the buyer is irrational.

**Transplant Test:** medicine has "compared to standard of care" and finance has "compared to opportunity cost" — structurally related, mechanistically different. The force is native only when it names *this institution's* live alternatives; swap the nouns and you must respecify the baseline, which is the test passing.

**Failure Mode:** feature superiority mistaken for decision relevance.

---

### FORCE II: The Complement-Bottleneck Force

**Surface Pattern:** a capable product is bought and underperforms; everyone blames the product or the users.

**Invisible Mechanism:** David Teece's *Profiting from Technological Innovation* shows value depends on **co-specialized complementary assets** — skills, data, infrastructure, support, maintenance — that arrive unevenly, and whoever controls the scarce complement captures the value.

**Second-Order Force:** the cheapest focal product routinely produces the most expensive *system*, and a complement owner can capture more value than the innovator.

**Application:** redefine the adopted unit as the *minimum viable institutional package*, not the artifact.

**Changes Diagnosis:** when adoption underdelivers, audit the missing complements before concluding the technology failed.

**Transplant Test:** "you need supporting infrastructure" exists everywhere, but the specific mechanism — co-specialized assets whose *owner captures the innovator's returns* — is native to how technologies distribute economic value. Move it to education and you must name the domain's own complements (teacher capability, materials, coaching); the force survives only by that renaming.

**Failure Mode:** training deployed as a ritual substitute for missing architecture.

---

### FORCE III: The Switching-Cost / Lock-In Force

**Surface Pattern:** an institution keeps an inferior arrangement or refuses a cheap, clearly better tool.

**Invisible Mechanism:** Ronald Coase's "The Nature of the Firm" and Oliver Williamson's transaction-cost economics locate the hidden bulk — search, negotiation, integration, monitoring, migration, and enforcement costs — while W. Brian Arthur's "Competing Technologies, Increasing Returns, and Lock-In" shows installed base and complements creating self-reinforcing returns around a prior choice.

**Second-Order Force:** early choices reshape later options, and hidden costs surface *after* commitment, when bargaining power has shifted to the incumbent.

**Application:** estimate total adoption, operation, switching, and exit cost across actors and time — not the license price.

**Changes Diagnosis:** when a superior tool is refused, price the switching cost before concluding the buyer is short-sighted.

**Transplant Test:** lock-in appears in language and law, but the specific mechanism — asset-specific transaction costs plus increasing returns to an installed technical base — is native to how organizations govern technology commitments. It cannot move to, say, friendship without losing its meaning.

**Failure Mode:** license price mistaken for institutional cost; "open" anything assumed to abolish dependence.

---

### FORCE IV: The Legitimacy-Decoupling Force

**Surface Pattern:** similar organizations adopt the same form at the same moment, sometimes before any evidence exists — then nothing changes on the floor.

**Invisible Mechanism:** DiMaggio and Powell's "The Iron Cage Revisited" names coercive, mimetic, and normative pressure; Meyer and Rowan's "Institutionalized Organizations" names the result — *ceremonial conformity with operational decoupling*, where a form is adopted to secure standing while practice is left untouched.

**Second-Order Force:** refusal starts to look illegitimate, so even harmful practices diffuse, and obedience is misread by leadership as endorsement.

**Application:** separate evidence-based conviction from mandate, imitation, professional expectation, and ceremony.

**Changes Diagnosis:** when adoption is fast and uniform across a field, ask whether it is legitimacy-seeking before crediting the evidence.

**Transplant Test:** conformity is universal, but the specific mechanism — organizations decoupling formal structure from activity to secure *institutional legitimacy* — is native to organizational fields. In an individual it would be a different phenomenon entirely.

**Failure Mode:** legitimacy reduced to messaging; a compliance artifact mistaken for operational practice.

---

### FORCE V: The Role-Displacement Force

**Surface Pattern:** the buyer approves while users resist, or users celebrate while governance blocks; aggregate value is positive yet adoption stalls and breeds resentment.

**Invisible Mechanism:** Jensen and Meckling's "Theory of the Firm" gives the principal–agent engine; the value *created* (Teece) may not be the value *captured*, and neither aligns with who carries the *burden* or the *risk*. Apparent resistance is often rational defense against an expected, business-case-hidden loss.

**Second-Order Force:** a coalition can secure commitment while exporting failure and labor to actors who were never in the room.

**Application:** keep four separate ledgers — value created, captured, received, exported — by actor and over time.

**Changes Diagnosis:** when adoption stalls despite positive ROI, find the actor bearing an uncompensated loss before calling the resistance irrational.

**Transplant Test:** agency problems exist across law and politics, but the specific mechanism — value/burden/risk landing on *different organizational roles* with different vetoes — is native to institutional decision structures. Collapse the roles to one owner-operator and the force disappears, which is the test passing.

**Failure Mode:** "stakeholder buy-in" that hides conflict and treats consultation as authority.

---

### FORCE VI: The Normalization-Labor Force

**Surface Pattern:** the launch succeeds, training happens, and six months later the practice has quietly reverted or is bypassed.

**Invisible Mechanism:** Carl May and Tracy Finch's Normalization Process Theory shows that a practice becomes ordinary only through collective *work* — sense-making, enrollment, performing the new routine, and appraising it — and the sociotechnical tradition shows automation *relocates* effort rather than removing it.

**Second-Order Force:** the hidden coordination, verification, and repair labor accumulates on frontline staff, degrading fidelity while the dashboard still shows "adopted."

**Application:** map before-and-after work by role, including double entry, exception handling, and repair; treat duty-borne use as *cost*, not acceptance.

**Changes Diagnosis:** when usage looks fine but value doesn't materialize, measure the exported workload before crediting normalization.

**Transplant Test:** "habits take work to form" is generic; the specific mechanism — the four kinds of collective *implementation work* by which a practice becomes legitimate ordinary activity in an organization — is native to institutional enactment. An individual habit does not require enrollment or appraisal by others.

**Failure Mode:** installed infrastructure (a national platform, a filled dashboard) mistaken for normalized practice.

---

### FORCE VII: The Threshold-Evidence Force

**Surface Pattern:** a persuasive pilot is asked to prove safety, scale, integration, and value-for-money it was never designed to establish — or, worse, is *not* asked, and its one result becomes universal proof.

**Invisible Mechanism:** each commitment threshold needs its own evidence — feasibility, comparative effect, readiness, reliability, distributional impact, sustainability, exit — and procurement tends to *freeze* assumptions before learning can correct them.

**Second-Order Force:** evidence gets overextended in one direction and frozen in the other, so the institution commits to a claim the pilot never tested.

**Application:** stage commitments with threshold-specific proof, bounded trials, independent review, and termination rights.

**Changes Diagnosis:** when a decision feels over- or under-evidenced, name which *threshold* it is at and what proof that threshold actually requires.

**Transplant Test:** "match evidence to stakes" is a general virtue; the specific mechanism — distinct proof burdens attached to distinct *organizational commitment thresholds*, frozen by procurement — is native to how institutions buy under irreversibility. It does not describe a personal choice.

**Failure Mode:** one testimonial, pilot, or compliance artifact treated as proof for every later threshold.

---

### FORCE VIII: The Exit-Absence Force

**Surface Pattern:** a program grows, then can no longer justify itself, yet cannot be stopped; or a dependency deepens until leaving is unaffordable.

**Invisible Mechanism:** WHO/ExpandNet's *Nine Steps for Developing a Scaling-Up Strategy* and the de-implementation literature treat horizontal spread, vertical embedding, financial continuity, and *retirement* as different systems — and without exit architecture (portability, migration, succession, recovery), adoption hardens into involuntary dependence.

**Second-Order Force:** scale magnifies fragility, sustainability becomes lock-in, and the absence of a decommissioning path converts a past commitment into a permanent tax.

**Application:** design portability, migration, archival duties, and recovery *before* commitment; track evidence freshness and decommissioning as first-class.

**Changes Diagnosis:** when a program persists, ask whether it still delivers value or merely cannot be exited.

**Transplant Test:** "plan your exit" is generic advice; the specific mechanism — the divergence of scale, sustainment, and exit as *distinct institutional systems* that produce involuntary dependence when the last is absent — is native to long-lived organizational commitments.

**Failure Mode:** continued funding or national presence mistaken for continued value.

## PART IV: THE STAGES OF THE ADOPTION CARTOGRAPHER

*The stages describe changes in perception, not prestige. Most practitioners stop at Stage 3.*

### Stage 1: The Feature Advocate
**Inner voice:** "The best product should win."
Sees the demo and its attributes. **Trap:** believing superiority is destiny. **Move:** for one real decision, write the live counterfactual the buyer is actually choosing among.

### Stage 2: The Acceptance Measurer
**Inner voice:** "Users love it, so we're winning."
Sees perceived usefulness, ease, and usage. **Trap:** reading sentiment as adoption. **Move:** trace one enthusiastic pilot to the budget, authorization, and complements it still lacks.

### Stage 3: The Buying-System Mapper
**Inner voice:** "I know who signs."
Sees gatekeepers, purchaser, and decision rights. **Trap:** treating persuasion as alignment; not yet seeing switching cost, distribution, or installed base.

### ⬇ THE THRESHOLD VALLEY — most commercial and program practitioners plateau here.
The valley does not feel like one: the deal closes, the pilot lands, everyone is congratulated. Nothing signals that value has not yet been created. The only way through is deliberate discomfort — follow one "successful" adoption six months past the signature and measure what reverted, who absorbed the workload, and what the dashboard hid.

### Stage 4: The Adoption Economist
**Inner voice:** "I can price the whole commitment."
Sees total cost, complements, incentives, and value distribution across actors. **Trap:** assuming economic fit guarantees enactment; not yet seeing legitimacy or ordinary work.

### Stage 5: The Implementation & Legitimacy Steward
**Inner voice:** "I can see whether the practice will become ordinary."
Sees normalization labor, fidelity, coalition, and ceremonial conformity. **Trap:** preserving the program instead of its value; not yet seeing evidence decay, lock-in, or de-adoption.

### Stage 6: The Lifecycle & Exit Governor
**Inner voice:** "I hold the whole arc, including the ending."
Sees the comparative portfolio, renewal, refusal, and retirement. **Trap:** treating the portfolio view as omniscience. **Move:** name what ground contact keeps the map honest — return to the field.

## PART V: THE TOOLKIT

**Tool 1 — Lifecycle Ledger.** Record diffusion, acceptance, commitment, procurement, implementation, normalization, scale, and sustainment/exit as *separate* claims. Never let one stand for another.

**Tool 2 — Counterfactual Card.** Compare the proposal against current practice, workaround, delay, refusal, rival, and internal build.

**Tool 3 — Adoption-Unit Diagram.** Draw the focal artifact with every complement it needs to function; the boundary of that drawing is the real thing being adopted.

**Tool 4 — Role-and-Rights Map.** Locate purchaser, gatekeeper, implementer, user, beneficiary, affected party, maintainer, accountable owner — and each one's veto and recourse.

**Tool 5 — Value-and-Burden Ledger.** Separate value created, captured, received, and exported, by actor and over time.

**Tool 6 — Workflow Delta Map.** Before-and-after work by role: double entry, oversight, exceptions, repair.

**Tool 7 — Legitimacy Field Map.** Distinguish evidence, coercion, imitation, professional norm, and ceremony.

**Tool 8 — Evidence Gate.** Match each irreversible commitment to a threshold-specific proof and a stop rule.

**Tool 9 — Exit & Decommission Plan.** Portability, migration, archival duties, recovery, accountability — written before commitment.

## PART VI: DIAGNOSTICS

Use these when an adoption looks healthy but you are unsure.

1. Which threshold is this actually at, and what proof does *that* threshold require?
2. Who pays, uses, benefits, bears risk, maintains, and can exit — and do any of them coincide?
3. What is the live counterfactual: current practice, workaround, delay, refusal, rival, internal build?
4. What complements must arrive for the artifact to function, and who controls them?
5. Where do value and burden land, by actor?
6. Is this adoption evidence-driven, or legitimacy-driven?
7. What would exit cost, concretely, if the bet is wrong?
8. Six months after "adoption," what reverted, and who absorbed the workload?

If question 8 has no answer, the adoption has not been observed — only announced.

## PART VII: CASE TRACES

### Case Trace 1: One Laptop per Child, Peru — Complement-Bottleneck (II)
Peru's large evaluation (Cristia et al.) found computer access and use rose while measured math and language learning did not. The hardware was distributed; the pedagogy, teacher capability, and classroom complements — the actual adoption unit — were not. **Forces:** Complement-Bottleneck (II), Threshold-Evidence (VII — access was proven, learning was assumed).

### Case Trace 2: NHS National Programme for IT, England — Switching-Cost & Threshold-Evidence
The UK National Audit Office traced how central contracts (Coase/Williamson costs made concrete), supplier concentration, and shifting requirements created scale commitments faster than any site could normalize them. **Forces:** Switching-Cost/Lock-In (III), Threshold-Evidence (VII), Normalization-Labor (VI).

### Case Trace 3: DHIS2, Kenya — Normalization-Labor (VI)
A national health-information platform was institutionally adopted and data were entered, yet accuracy, interpretation, and decision-use stayed uneven. Installed infrastructure is not normalized practice. **Forces:** Normalization-Labor (VI), Legitimacy-Decoupling (IV — presence without enacted use).

### Case Trace 4: Tusome, Kenya — the constructive counter-case
Tusome scaled because it carried its complements — structured teacher guides, coaching, monitoring, and government delivery machinery — not because an idea diffused. It is what Complement-Bottleneck and Normalization-Labor look like when *solved*. **Forces:** Complement-Bottleneck (II) resolved, Normalization-Labor (VI) resourced.

### Case Trace 5: VHS vs Betamax — Switching-Cost / Lock-In (III)
Recording duration, licensing, and complement ecosystems — not picture quality — decided the standard. The durable lesson is increasing returns to an installed base, and the corrective to the folklore that "inferior technology always wins." **Forces:** Switching-Cost/Lock-In (III).

## PART IX: THE PRESSURE-REPRESENTATION CROSSING

> K2M synthesis — not a construct claimed by the sources above.

Deadline, budget expiry, status, safety, regulator expectation, vendor urgency, sunk cost, and overload act on an institution *before* the visible request. A representation earns its place when it makes those pressures inspectable without becoming hidden persuasion. The adoption artist therefore represents the **threshold** (each requires different proof), the **constellation** (the speaker is rarely the payer, risk-bearer, or accountable owner), the **counterfactual** (delay, workaround, refusal, rival, internal build), the **package** (complements and hidden work belong inside the unit), the **distribution** (value, burden, risk, recourse by actor), the **reversible commitment** (a bounded trial with stop rules can be truer than a full promise), the **ordinary work** (schedules, exceptions, repair), and the **exit** (portability, migration, decommissioning). For a principal, a one-page threshold map — learning value, roles, teacher workload, safeguarding, evidence gaps, pilot limits, recourse, and the way out — restores the structure of the decision without taking the decision away.

## CREED: THE INSTITUTIONAL ADOPTION CARTOGRAPHER'S OATH

I will not mistake the signature for the commitment.

I will not mistake the login for the practice.

I will not mistake reach for value.

I will name the counterfactual before I claim advantage.

I will find the actor who carries the burden before I call the resistance irrational.

I will draw the whole package, not the artifact.

I will price the switching cost and the exit before I build the dependence.

I will ask which threshold I am at, and demand the proof that threshold requires.

I will follow the adoption past the launch, to where the practice lives or dies.
