# THE ATTENTION AND PERCEPTION CARTOGRAPHER'S MANIFESTO
## A Map of Why the World Is Not Equally Available — and What the Eye Cannot See

---

> The map is not the territory.
>
> But in perception, the territory you experience is already a map — selected, organized, and edited before you know it.
>
> Every scene presents itself as simply "there." The master sees the selection that built it.
>
> This document maps that selection.

---

## PREAMBLE: THE DOMINANT MISUNDERSTANDING

You believe perception records what is present and attention is the willpower to keep looking.

If an object was visible, the person saw it. If they looked at it, they understood it. If they understood it, they could choose well. If they failed, their attention span was weak. Seeing is receiving; attention is effort.

This is what the surface teaches, because from the inside experience feels complete — a seamless world, fully present, faithfully received.

But the world is not equally available. Attention is a family of selection processes, not a tank of effort. Perception organizes a field into figures, objects, streams, and opportunities for action — it does not deliver an exhaustive inner photograph. Large changes go unreported. Eyes fixate without comprehension. Expertise makes a faint pattern immediate while leaving an unrelated search ordinary.

**Mastery is NOT:**
- Making something noticeable and calling the job done
- Believing a fixation proves attention, or attention proves awareness
- Treating "attention span" as one trait
- Reading a heat map as a display of comprehension or preference
- Blaming the person for a mismatch the design created

**Mastery IS:**
- Mapping the conditions under which a signal becomes selectable, organized, inspectable, actionable, and corrigible for *this* receptor
- Keeping gaze, attention, awareness, comprehension, choice, and learning strictly separate
- Auditing the five priority signals before making a cue louder
- Designing for plural bodies, not the assumed normal receptor
- Returning the first sight to the field so a second look can correct it

## PART I: THE FOUR LAYERS OF THE PERCEPTUAL FIELD

### Layer 1: The Visible
Fixations, missed targets, reaction times, selected buttons, scan paths, recalled items, verbal reports, errors, completed tasks.
**What lives here:** eye-tracking output, click data, recall scores.
**The trap:** observable, but its meaning is underdetermined — a fixation is not comprehension.

### Layer 2: The Mechanics
Alerting, covert orienting, filtering, feature binding, grouping, target templates, guided search, auditory streams, scene gist, masking, motor exploration.
**What lives here:** the selection and organization operations beneath the trace.
**The trap:** mechanics without dynamics produce technicians who tune a cue and cannot see why it fails for a different goal or body.

### Layer 3: The Dynamics
Selection history, perceptual learning, reward, prevalence, fatigue, switching, arousal, adaptation, expertise, feedback, and recursive changes in what the observer expects.
**What lives here:** how priority shifts over time and with state.
**The trap:** dynamics without invisible forces produce analysts who see effects vary and cannot name the forces.

### Layer 4: The Invisible
The assumed normal receptor, the task set, the scene ontology, institutional definitions of evidence, unmeasured sensory access, cultural experience, authority over pacing, and the inference that turns a gaze trace into a claim about a mind.
**What lives here:** the native forces of Part III.
Most "attention" failures begin when visible-layer evidence is used to invent an invisible-layer cause without tracing the mechanics between.

## PART III: THE TEN NATIVE FORCES

### FORCE I: Priority Convergence
**Surface Pattern:** one item grabs attention while another stays technically visible but practically absent.
**Invisible Mechanism:** physical salience, current goals, selection history, learned value, and scene syntax converge into a changing priority order (Wolfe's guided search; Awh/Belopolsky/Theeuwes).
**Second-Order Force:** repeated selection trains future priority; a formerly useful cue keeps winning after the goal changed.
**Application:** audit all five priority sources before redesigning a cue.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when something is missed, ask what outranked it in the priority map before assuming it was invisible.
**Transplant Test:** "we prioritize" is generic; the specific mechanism — a *perceptual priority map* fed by five specific signals governing selection — is native to attention science.
**Failure Mode:** universal color/motion rules that mistake one input for the whole system.

### FORCE II: The Objecthood Gate
**Surface Pattern:** parts are noticed together, or sounds fuse into one stream, before the observer decides what belongs.
**Invisible Mechanism:** grouping by continuity, proximity, common onset, and similarity builds the *units* attention can select (Wertheimer; object-based attention).
**Second-Order Force:** a stable grouping becomes the apparent world; a wrong grouping fuses unrelated evidence or splits one movement.
**Application:** test alternative figure/ground and auditory/visual groupings.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a reading is wrong at the root, check the grouping before the reasoning.
**Transplant Test:** "we chunk things" is loose; the specific mechanism — *perceptual grouping* forming the selectable object prior to choice — is native to Gestalt/object-based attention.
**Failure Mode:** "my failure" and "the constraint" fused into one perceived object.

### FORCE III: Binding Demand
**Surface Pattern:** a simple feature pops out, while the correct conjunction is slow and error-prone.
**Invisible Mechanism:** features (color, orientation, location, motion) must be bound into an object; detailed recognition often needs selective processing (Treisman & Gelade, revised by guided search).
**Second-Order Force:** visual emphasis manufactures false conjunctions — the right color on the wrong label, or an arrow binding two events into unsupported causality.
**Application:** separate feature detection from relation verification; test for misbinding.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a false relation is seen, check whether emphasis created a false binding.
**Transplant Test:** "connect the pieces" is generic; the specific mechanism — *feature binding* into an object under attentional load — is native to visual search.
**Failure Mode:** resonant elements placed near each other mistaken for an inspectable relation.

### FORCE IV: Load–Selection Crossover
**Surface Pattern:** added demand sometimes blocks distraction and sometimes makes it worse.
**Invisible Mechanism:** high *perceptual* load can consume capacity that would process distractors, while high *working-memory/control* load weakens goal maintenance.
**Second-Order Force:** "reduce cognitive load" becomes meaningless when sensory density, memory, switching, and response are treated as one quantity.
**Application:** name the exact load and the operation it taxes.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when simplification fails, ask which load it removed and which it left.
**Transplant Test:** "don't overload people" is generic; the specific mechanism — *load type* determining whether distraction is blocked or amplified — is native to selective attention.
**Failure Mode:** removing useful structure while leaving the interpretive control load untouched.

### FORCE V: Search-Termination Threshold
**Surface Pattern:** rare or unexpected targets are missed even by motivated observers.
**Invisible Mechanism:** prevalence, expectation, and quitting criteria determine when search ends; search is guided and satisficing, not exhaustive (Wolfe's low-prevalence effect).
**Second-Order Force:** a missed target is reread as absent; lowered expectation creates a self-reinforcing blind spot.
**Application:** add rare-target checks and permission to reopen a concluded search.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a target is "not there," check the stopping rule before trusting the absence.
**Transplant Test:** "we give up early" is loose; the specific mechanism — *prevalence and quitting thresholds* in guided visual search — is native to search research.
**Failure Mode:** the hidden move dismissed as beyond capacity when it was outside the search template.

### FORCE VI: Gist–Focal Calibration
**Surface Pattern:** an expert registers that something is wrong almost immediately, then inspects fewer, better locations.
**Invisible Mechanism:** domain-specific perceptual learning builds global templates that guide focal verification (radiological gist; Sheridan & Reingold).
**Second-Order Force:** fast gist becomes unearned authority; novice fluency mistaken for expertise.
**Application:** pair rapid orientation with explicit focal checks and feedback from labeled variation.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a snap judgment is trusted, ask whether it is trained gist or fluent guessing.
**Transplant Test:** "experts see fast" is generic; the specific mechanism — *holistic perceptual gist* guiding focal search in a practiced visual domain — is native to expertise research.
**Failure Mode:** recognition-first orientation mistaken for verified sight.

### FORCE VII: Transient Dependence
**Surface Pattern:** a large change is missed when a cut, blank, or redesign interrupts comparison.
**Invisible Mechanism:** change detection often depends on a local transient; disruption masks the cue that would route attention to the change (Rensink/O'Regan/Clark flicker).
**Second-Order Force:** before/after artifacts look dramatic yet fail to reveal what moved when scale, position, or labels also changed.
**Application:** preserve comparison anchors, align versions, and cue the changed relation.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a change is missed, check whether the transient was removed.
**Transplant Test:** "people miss changes" is loose; the specific mechanism — *transient-dependent* change detection defeated by a blank or cut — is native to change-blindness research.
**Failure Mode:** a new version admired without the person detecting what actually changed.

### FORCE VIII: Action-Scaled Affordance
**Surface Pattern:** the same stair, control, or proposed move is actionable for one person and unavailable to another.
**Invisible Mechanism:** affordances arise in the relation among environment, body, capability, skill, and current state; perception is organized for possible action (Gibson; Warren's climbable stairs).
**Second-Order Force:** designers call a move "small" because it is conceptually simple while ignoring time, sensory access, exposure, or cost.
**Application:** test whether the move can be found, executed, stopped, reversed, and followed by usable feedback.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when a "simple" move fails, check whether it is a real affordance for this body.
**Transplant Test:** "it depends on the person" is generic; the specific mechanism — a body-scaled *affordance* as a relation for action — is native to ecological perception.
**Failure Mode:** a next move that is not actually available in the live field.

### FORCE IX: Arousal Priority Amplification
**Surface Pattern:** under arousal or stress, some information dominates while other information weakens; one task improves and another degrades.
**Invisible Mechanism:** arousal amplifies already-high-priority representations and suppresses competitors; acute stress affects working memory and flexibility unevenly (Mather & Sutherland; Shields et al.).
**Second-Order Force:** "stress narrows attention" becomes a universal tunnel-vision myth hiding enhancement, heterogeneity, and time course.
**Application:** identify the prior priority and the affected operation before predicting impairment.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when stress "hurt performance," ask which operation and which prior priority, not "attention" in general.
**Transplant Test:** "stress changes focus" is loose; the specific mechanism — *arousal-biased competition* amplifying existing perceptual priority — is native to the arousal/attention literature.
**Failure Mode:** a universal narrowing law asserted over a conditional, heterogeneous effect.

### FORCE X: Receptor–Environment Fit
**Surface Pattern:** a representation is "clear" to its assumed observer and inaccessible, exhausting, or ambiguous to another.
**Invisible Mechanism:** perceivability depends on sensory channel, embodiment, language, culture, timing, technology, and environment; disability can be a material *misfit*, not a deficit (Garland-Thomson; Hamraie).
**Second-Order Force:** the normal receptor becomes invisible, people are blamed for a design mismatch, and conformance is mistaken for access.
**Application:** provide alternatives, user control, assistive-technology structure, time, and testing with affected communities.
**Changes Diagnosis:** when someone "can't use it," locate the misfit before locating a deficit.
**Transplant Test:** "design for everyone" is a slogan; the specific mechanism — *perceptual fit* as a body–environment relation where disability is misfit — is native to perception and disability studies.
**Failure Mode:** the assumed normal receptor treated as universal.

## PART IV: THE PERCEPTUAL STAGES OF THE ATTENTION CARTOGRAPHER

### Stage 1: The Camera Believer
**Inner voice:** "If it was there, I saw it."
Assumes perception records what is present. **Trap:** missed information read as carelessness. **Move:** study inattentional and change blindness.

### Stage 2: The Spotlight Operator
**Inner voice:** "I just have to focus harder."
Sees limited attention and spatial orienting. **Trap:** treating attention as one voluntary beam and gaze as proof. **Move:** study covert orienting and object-based selection.

### Stage 3: The Priority Reader
**Inner voice:** "Goals, salience, history, reward, and scene compete."
Sees the priority map. **Trap:** converting it into universal design recipes. **Move:** study guided search, load crossover, and stopping rules.

### ⬇ THE RECEPTOR VALLEY — most designers and researchers plateau here.
The valley is invisible because the work *tests well* — on the assumed observer, in the lab, on the desktop. Nothing signals that the "clear" design excludes a different body, or that a conditional effect has been frozen into a law about a person. Crossing it means designing for plural receptors and treating measurement humility as part of the craft.

### Stage 4: The Organization Cartographer
**Inner voice:** "Grouping, binding, and gist build what I select."
Sees Gestalt organization and objecthood. **Trap:** applying Gestalt tendencies as laws and confusing fluency with truth. **Move:** study cultural variation and the penetrability debate.

### Stage 5: The Embodied Perceiver
**Inner voice:** "Perception is for action, in a body."
Sees movement, environment, and affordance. **Trap:** romanticizing direct perception or ignoring institutions. **Move:** study ecological, sensorimotor, and predictive disagreements.

### Stage 6: The State & Expertise Calibrator
**Inner voice:** "Training, arousal, prevalence, and feedback change priority conditionally."
Sees conditional effects. **Trap:** turning conditional effects into labels about a person. **Move:** study meta-analysis, replication, and measurement validity.

### Stage 7: The Plural-Receptor Artist
**Inner voice:** "Build multimodal, task-bounded, correctable perceptual fields."
Believes neither reflexivity nor compliance completes access; sustains co-design, community authority, and longitudinal field return.

## PART V: THE TOOLKIT

**Tool 1 — Attention-Operation Card.** Name alerting, orienting, selecting, sustaining, switching, inhibition, comparison, or memory.
**Tool 2 — Five-Signal Priority Audit.** Physical salience, goal, history, learned value, scene meaning.
**Tool 3 — Objecthood Test.** What has the medium grouped into one selectable unit?
**Tool 4 — Gist–Focal Pair.** One orientation surface and one inspectable-evidence surface.
**Tool 5 — Rare-Target Check.** The high-consequence signal the search is likely to quit before finding.
**Tool 6 — Before/After Anchor Test.** Stabilize position, scale, labels, and reference points.
**Tool 7 — Pressure-Condition Matrix.** Separate arousal, acute stress, anxiety, scarcity, fatigue, shame, authority exposure.
**Tool 8 — Eye–Mind Inference Ladder.** Add claims beyond fixation only with independent evidence.
**Tool 9 — Affordance Viability Test.** Can the person find, execute, stop, reverse, and get feedback?
**Tool 10 — Receptor-Fit Interview.** Modality, pace, sensory environment, language, assistive technology, timing.

## PART VI: DIAGNOSTICS

1. Which attention operation is actually in play — alerting, orienting, selecting, sustaining, switching, comparison, memory?
2. Which of the five priority signals is driving selection here?
3. What has the medium grouped into one selectable unit?
4. Is this evidence about gaze, attention, awareness, comprehension, choice, or learning — and am I inferring beyond it?
5. What high-consequence rare target might the search quit before finding?
6. Are the before/after anchors stable enough to detect what changed?
7. Which stress/arousal state, and which operation, is affected — not "attention" in general?
8. Can this person find, execute, stop, reverse, and get feedback from the move?
9. What second look, in which modality, would correct the first?

## PART VII: CASE TRACES

**Cherry's cocktail-party experiments (1953)** — selective auditory streaming: follow one message amid competition, report little of the other; poor report ≠ no processing. **Forces:** Priority Convergence (I), Objecthood Gate (II).

**Posner's covert-orienting paradigm** — attention shifts to a cued location while fixation stays put; valid cues help, invalid cost. **Forces:** breaks gaze=attention; Priority Convergence (I).

**Simons & Chabris's gorilla** — counting passes, observers miss an unexpected gorilla: task-set exclusion, not defective eyesight. **Forces:** Priority Convergence (I), Search-Termination (V).

**Rensink, O'Regan & Clark's flicker** — a blank between scenes hides large changes. **Forces:** Transient Dependence (VII).

**Warren's climbable stairs** — judged climbability scaled with bodily dimensions; the affordance is relational. **Forces:** Action-Scaled Affordance (VIII).

**Radiological gist + rare-target countercase** — experts detect abnormal structure in a fraction of a second, yet rare targets are missed disproportionately. **Forces:** Gist–Focal Calibration (VI), Search-Termination (V).

**Himba local processing (Davidoff et al.)** — a strong local bias challenges universal global-precedence; task-bound, not a permanent "style." **Forces:** Objecthood Gate (II), Receptor–Environment Fit (X).

### Source-Bias Risks & Unresolved
- Experimental psychology overrepresents brief lab tasks and Western, educated samples; acute lab stress is not poverty, shame, or institutional threat; vision research often assumes a sighted desktop observer.
- Reject dopamine mythology, attention-span monoculture, learning-styles matching, and heat-map literalism.
- **Unresolved:** whether Objecthood and Binding are one force; how much Arousal Amplification generalizes beyond lab arousal; where the attention-economy province must be joined.

## PART IX: THE PRESSURE-REPRESENTATION CROSSING

> K2M synthesis — directional design inferences, not findings claimed by the cited traditions.

Ask what already outranks your representation — deadline, authority voice, shame cue, familiar script — before making it louder (Priority Convergence). If "my failure" and "the institution's constraint" have fused into one perceived object, no highlighting restores authorship until the grouping changes (Objecthood). Make the force–trace relation *inspectable* rather than placing resonant elements near each other (Binding). Reopen a closed search when the hidden move sits outside the current template, without promising every option is findable (Search-Termination). Preserve enough continuity that the person can detect how the map changed after acting (Transient Dependence). A next move is valid only while it stays a real affordance in the live field. And the invisible need not become visual — it must become manipulable through a channel the person can use and contest (Receptor Fit). No representation earns sovereignty because it captured attention or produced recognition; the first sight is a hypothesis organized by a task and a receptor. Return it to the field.

## CREED: THE ATTENTION AND PERCEPTION CARTOGRAPHER'S OATH

I will not mistake a fixation for attention, or attention for understanding.

I will not treat the world as equally available.

I will audit the five priority signals before I make a cue louder.

I will not read a heat map as a window into a mind.

I will name the load, the operation, and the state — not "attention" in general.

I will design for plural bodies, not the receptor I happen to be.

I will not turn a conditional effect into a verdict about a person.

I will preserve the anchors that let a change be seen.

I will return the first sight to the field.

The mature cartographer does not capture attention. They create the conditions for a second look — and make sure the second look can correct the first.
